      </li>
      <li>

<section className="section" id="measure">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="measure">1) Measure the funnel: no data, no CRO</h2>
    <p>
      CRO is a decision discipline. Your job is to find where the funnel leaks, then fix the most likely cause. On Shopify, the most useful funnel is:
    </p>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Product view</strong> → <strong>Add to cart</strong> → <strong>Checkout started</strong> → <strong>Purchase</strong>
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>A. KPIs (and what they imply)</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Add-to-cart rate:</strong> offer/price issue, unclear product value, weak trust, or poor variant UX.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Checkout start:</strong> cart friction (surprise fees) or CTA visibility issues.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Checkout completion:</strong> payment/shipping friction, form pain, trust gaps, bugs.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>RPV (revenue per visitor):</strong> the best single KPI to compare two versions.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. The “3-page” method</h3>
    <p>
      If time is limited: pick <strong>three pages</strong> that combine traffic and potential (often 2 collections + 1 hero product). Don’t spread effort across 50 pages.
    </p>
    <p className="note">
      A page with impressions but low clicks is often a <strong>SEO</strong> problem (snippet/CTR). A page with clicks but low purchases is a <strong>CRO</strong> problem. Fix both in sequence.
    </p>

    <h3>C. A quick diagnostic (to avoid guessing)</h3>
    <p>
      Before changing anything, answer these questions on your top pages. They usually point to the real bottleneck faster than “random ideas.”
    </p>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        Do users bounce before seeing the CTA (speed/layout shift issue)?
      </li>
      <li>
        Do they scroll but not add to cart (offer/clarity/trust issue)?
      </li>
      <li>
        Do they add to cart but abandon in cart (fee surprise, shipping uncertainty, promo code distraction)?
      </li>
      <li>
        Do they start checkout but fail to complete (payment/shipping friction, form pain, trust gap, bug)?
      </li>
      <li>
        Do they buy once but never return (product experience, delivery, support, retention flows)?
      </li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</section>

      </li>
      <li>

<section className="section" id="product-page">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="product-page">3) Product page: remove objections in the right order</h2>
    <p>
      A product page converts when it answers one question: <em>“Is this for me, right now?”</em> Your job is to reduce uncertainty (quality, sizing, delivery, returns) and prove value (reviews, use, details).
    </p>

    <h3>A. The “decision block” (above the fold)</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Title</strong> (clear) + <strong>price</strong> + <strong>variants</strong> + <strong>CTA</strong> (no distractions).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Max 3 benefits</strong>, written for use (“durable,” “lightweight,” “compatible”).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Shipping/returns</strong> in one line (typical delivery time + return window).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Express payments</strong> visible (if enabled) to reduce friction.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. The “proof block” (right after)</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Reviews:</strong> rating + volume + 3 highlighted reviews (ideally with photos).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>UGC:</strong> 4–8 real-life photos or use cases (not only studio images).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Product FAQ:</strong> sizing, compatibility, care, warranty, returns.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>C. The “details block” (for rational buyers)</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Specs</strong> (materials, dimensions, weight, what’s included, compatibility).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Comparison</strong> (optional): standard vs premium, size differences, model differences.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Warranty &amp; support</strong>: clear process, response times, contact.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <p className="note">
      Rule of thumb: if you can’t express your value proposition in one sentence plus three benefits, the product page won’t compensate. Fix the offer first.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section className="section" id="collections">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="collections">4) Collections: merchandising, filters and guided choice</h2>
    <p>
      A collection maps a need to an assortment. A converting collection is not just a grid—it helps users choose.
    </p>

    <h3>A. What to add (often missing)</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Short intro</strong> (100–200 words): who it’s for, best use cases, how to choose.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>2–3 filters</strong> at first (avoid complexity overload).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Badges</strong> (best-seller, new, fast shipping) only if true.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Sorting</strong>: best-sellers by default, not alphabetical.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. Collection mistakes that kill conversion</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Unreadable grid</strong> (inconsistent images, unclear prices, messy hierarchy).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Hidden promises</strong> (free shipping only revealed at checkout).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Infinite facets</strong> (too many choices slows decisions).
      </li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</section>

<section className="section" id="cart">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="cart">5) Cart: the final checkpoint before payment</h2>
    <p>
      Many stores talk about checkout and ignore the cart. But the cart is where buyers verify that everything is coherent:
      <strong> price</strong>, <strong>quantity</strong>, <strong>delivery time</strong>, <strong>returns</strong> and <strong>payment</strong>. Missing info creates hesitation; surprises create abandonment.
    </p>

    <h3>A. What the cart must answer in under 10 seconds</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Is the total clear?</strong> subtotal, shipping estimate (or rule), taxes where applicable.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Is delivery credible?</strong> date estimate or time window (“ships in 24–48h,” “delivered in 2–4 days”).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Is risk low?</strong> return policy, warranty, support contact.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Will payment be easy?</strong> show express payment options without forcing.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. Elements that often increase conversion (without dark patterns)</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Shipping/returns reminder:</strong> 1–2 lines plus a link to details.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Free-shipping threshold:</strong> a discreet indicator (“only $X more”) only if margin allows.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Minimal upsell:</strong> one extremely relevant add-on; otherwise, nothing.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Stable cart UI:</strong> avoid heavy animations that move the CTA.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Cart drawer vs cart page:</strong> drawers are fast for adding; a dedicated cart page is better for decision-making.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>C. Common mistakes</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Promo code field too prominent:</strong> it encourages leaving to “find a coupon.”
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Too many cross-sells:</strong> cognitive overload and slower pages.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Hidden fees:</strong> revealed late or described vaguely (“calculated at next step”).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Secondary CTA is louder:</strong> “continue shopping” dominates “checkout.”
      </li>
    </ul>

    <p className="note">
      High cart abandonment is rarely solved by button colour. Start with transparency (costs + delivery) and trust (returns + support).
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section className="section" id="checkout">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="checkout">6) Checkout: reduce friction (and surprises)</h2>
    <p>
      Most checkout abandonment comes from three causes: <strong>unexpected costs</strong>, <strong>payment friction</strong> and <strong>low trust</strong>. Checkout must be predictable—the customer should know what will happen before paying.
    </p>

    <h3>A. The 7 most reliable checkout levers</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Show fees early:</strong> shipping and taxes estimated from cart if possible.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Shop Pay / express pay:</strong> enable where it fits your market.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Trust:</strong> payment badges, returns policy, contact visible.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Short forms:</strong> limit fields, use autocomplete, clear error states.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Shipping options:</strong> 2–3 clear options, not 8.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Promo codes:</strong> if overemphasised, they encourage leaving checkout to search coupons.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Post-purchase offers:</strong> soft, relevant upsells after payment—not before.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <p className="note">
      Avoid brittle hacks. Shopify’s checkout evolves quickly; fragile modifications break on updates and create conversion bugs. Prefer stable, supported approaches.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section className="section" id="offers">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="offers">7) Offers &amp; pricing: the most powerful lever (if you own it)</h2>
    <p>
      CRO is not only UI. Often it’s the offer. Before stacking apps, test “business” levers: bundles, free-shipping thresholds, warranties, delivery promises.
    </p>

    <h3>A. 5 offer levers that often increase RPV</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Bundle:</strong> simpler than aggressive cross-sells; increases AOV.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Free-shipping threshold:</strong> calibrated to margin and typical baskets.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Clear warranty:</strong> reduces perceived risk.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Honest scarcity:</strong> real stock signals (no fake urgency).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Entry offer:</strong> a “starter” product to start the relationship.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. Messaging &amp; price transparency: reduce “mental friction”</h3>
    <p>
      Many conversion drops aren’t caused by a missing button—they’re caused by uncertainty. If users have to do mental math (“How much is shipping?” “Can I return it?” “When will it arrive?”),
      they postpone the decision. Transparency is a CRO lever because it replaces uncertainty with predictability.
    </p>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Reveal the true cost early:</strong> show shipping rules or estimated fees in cart (and on product pages when possible). Avoid “calculated at checkout” ambiguity.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Make the tradeoff explicit:</strong> if you charge for shipping but ship faster, say it clearly. If you ship free but slower, say it clearly.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Turn policies into benefits:</strong> “30-day returns” is a benefit when it is visible and easy to understand.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Use bundles to discount without training coupon-hunting:</strong> bundles feel like value, while constant discounts can hurt perceived quality.
      </li>
    </ul>
    <p className="note">
      A practical rule: if a user asks the same question in support (delivery time, compatibility, returns), surface the answer on the product page and cart.
      Reducing support tickets often correlates with higher conversion.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section className="section" id="trust">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="trust">8) Trust: the hidden variable behind conversion</h2>
    <p>
      Buyers in 2026 are exposed to opportunistic stores and scams. Trust is therefore the #1 conversion multiplier. Your store should feel serious: clarity, transparency, proof and service.
    </p>

    <h3>A. Trust checklist</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Shipping/returns</strong> visible on product pages and cart.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Contact</strong>: email, response time, support FAQ.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Legal pages</strong> accessible (footer).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Proof</strong>: reviews, UGC, press, partners—only if real.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Quality</strong>: consistent photography, precise descriptions, no vague promises.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. Support &amp; returns: the trust engine behind conversion</h3>
    <p>
      Trust is not a badge—it’s an experience. In ecommerce, the fastest way to build trust is to make “what happens if it goes wrong” obvious and painless.
      When returns are clear and support is reachable, buyers feel safe to proceed.
    </p>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Set expectations:</strong> show realistic delivery windows and proactive order updates (confirmation → shipping → tracking).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Make contact easy:</strong> one support email, one FAQ, and a promised response time beats five channels with no clarity.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Write “human” return rules:</strong> a short summary (“30 days, unused, original packaging”) plus the full policy.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Handle edge cases:</strong> gifts, exchanges, damaged items—address the common fears in a small FAQ.
      </li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</section>

    <h2 id="testing">9) Testing &amp; iteration: improve without fooling yourself</h2>

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Segmentation:</strong> mobile vs desktop (results often diverge).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Quality:</strong> monitor returns, support tickets, reviews. A “toxic” conversion lift destroys LTV.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. 10 high-value test ideas (Shopify)</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        Sticky CTA on mobile (product page).
      </li>
      <li>
        Three benefits above CTA vs longer copy.
      </li>
      <li>
        Shipping/returns block above the fold.
      </li>
      <li>
        UGC photos above the fold vs studio-only.
      </li>
      <li>
        Simple bundle vs multiple cross-sells.
      </li>
      <li>
        Best-seller sorting on collection vs default sorting.
      </li>
      <li>
        Express payment emphasis (button placement).
      </li>
      <li>
        Hide promo code behind link vs field visible.
      </li>
      <li>
        Product FAQ (5 questions) vs no FAQ.
      </li>
      <li>
        CTA copy (“Add to cart” vs “Buy now”) depending on product type.
      </li>
    </ul>
  </div>

<section className="section" id="faq-article">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="faq-article">FAQ — Shopify conversion (March 2026)</h2>

    <h3>Should I use “Buy now” or “Add to cart”?</h3>
    <p>
      It depends. For a simple, repeatable purchase, “Buy now” can reduce a step. For products with variants/accessories, “Add to cart” helps users compose and compare.
    </p>
