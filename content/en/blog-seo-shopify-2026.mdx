<section className="section" id="technical-foundations">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="technical-foundations">1) Technical foundations: indexation, canonicals, duplication</h2>
    <p>
      Shopify ships with a strong baseline (CDN, HTTPS, sitemaps), but most SEO drops still come from the same trio:
      <strong> duplicate URLs</strong>, <strong>low-value pages getting indexed</strong> and <strong>confusing internal linking</strong>.
      The goal is not to have “more pages,” but to focus crawl budget and authority on pages that sell.
    </p>

    <h3>A. Validate indexation (and use the right metrics)</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Search Console:</strong> track “Indexed pages,” “Crawled—currently not indexed,” 404s and redirects. A rise in “discovered”
        URLs is not a win if they never earn clicks.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Directory view:</strong> products, collections, pages, blog. Ecommerce SEO often wins on <em>collections</em> (commercial intent)
        plus <em>products</em> (transactional intent).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Focus:</strong> pick 10 key collections and ~50 strategic products. SEO is a focus game.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. Canonicals: your guardrail against URL variants</h3>
    <p>
      In Shopify, the same product can be accessible through multiple paths (collection context, variants, parameters). The{' '}
      <strong>canonical tag</strong> tells Google which version is preferred. A consistent canonical strategy prevents authority dilution.
    </p>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Rule:</strong> 1 search intent = 1 indexable URL. Sort/filter variants should usually remain non-indexed.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Collections:</strong> avoid creating multiple categories that mean the same thing unless you have a genuinely different assortment or angle.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>C. Faceted navigation & parameters: Shopify’s #1 SEO trap</h3>
    <p>
      Filters improve UX but can explode into thousands of URLs (colour, size, price, stock). Indexing them rarely helps early on:
      Google crawls a lot… and values little.
    </p>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Principle:</strong> keep filters for users, but <em>control</em> what can be indexed.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Red flag:</strong> Search Console shows hundreds of “Duplicate” or “Alternate page with proper canonical tag” URLs triggered by parameters.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Progressive approach:</strong> start with a clean index, then “open” only a few facets if you can create truly unique value (e.g., dedicated pages for specific use-cases).
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>D. Blog & informational pages: useful—but only with a plan</h3>
    <p>
      A blog without strategy becomes a pile of pretty pages that don’t sell. The right use in 2026: publish content that answers
      questions <em>before</em> purchase, and link that content to collections/products.
    </p>
    <p>
      If you’re starting from zero, build one hub article plus 4–6 supporting articles. For an overview of Shopify’s strengths and tradeoffs,
      read <a href="/en/blog-shopify">our Shopify review (2025)</a>. In this guide, we’ll focus on building the SEO engine around your store.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section className="section" id="performance">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="performance">2) Speed &amp; Core Web Vitals: the “double impact” lever (SEO + conversion)</h2>
    <p>
      Performance is not a “tech” topic—it’s a <strong>business</strong> topic. A faster store (1) is easier to crawl, (2) converts better.
      In 2026 this matters even more because marketing stacks keep getting heavier.
    </p>

    <h3>A. What makes Shopify stores slow (in 80% of cases)</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>App stacking:</strong> popups, upsells, reviews, tracking, chat—each adds JS/CSS and network requests.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Unoptimised images:</strong> oversized, non-responsive, too many above-the-fold assets.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Fonts &amp; animations:</strong> too many font variants, blocking loads, costly effects.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Overdesigned theme:</strong> huge DOM, sliders everywhere, heavy section logic.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. Performance checklist (without breaking your marketing)</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Inventory your stack:</strong> list apps, scripts (GTM, pixels, widgets) and measure impact (PageSpeed + WebPageTest). Keep what has proven ROI.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Images:</strong> compress, use responsive sizes, reduce carousels, prioritise one strong hero image instead of many average ones.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Loading:</strong> defer non-critical scripts, lazy-load below the fold, preload only truly essential assets.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Theme:</strong> pick a mobile-first, maintained theme with a clean baseline. A good theme beats a flashy theme plus 12 apps.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <p className="note">
      For the conversion side (product pages, cart, checkout), read: <a href="/en/blog-optimiser-conversion-shopify-2026">Shopify conversion optimization in 2026</a>.
      SEO and CRO are allies when you design for experience.
    </p>

    <h3>C. Core Web Vitals: use them, don’t obsess</h3>
    <p>
      Aim for consistency, not perfection. If you improve perceived speed (fast interaction, stable layout), you also improve engagement signals
      and reduce friction—helping both SEO and revenue.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section className="section" id="on-page">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="on-page">3) On-page SEO: products, collections and key pages</h2>
    <p>
      On Shopify, on-page SEO is not about stuffing keywords. It’s about aligning{' '}
      <strong>search intent</strong> ↔ <strong>page type</strong> ↔ <strong>proof</strong> (content, reviews, specs, shipping, returns).
    </p>

    <h3>A. Collections: your highest-leverage SEO pages</h3>
    <p>
      A collection is a category page: it should explain what users will find and how to choose. A strong collection page in 2026 includes:
    </p>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>A clear H1:</strong> “Waterproof hiking boots,” not “Collection #12.”
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Helpful copy:</strong> 150–300 words (top or bottom) with buying criteria, materials, use-cases and care tips.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Internal links:</strong> to 3–6 sub-collections or guides (“how to choose,” “size guide,” “care guide”).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Sane sorting:</strong> best-sellers first, then new arrivals, then price.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. Product pages: the goal is objection removal</h3>
    <p>
      Product pages rank on highly transactional queries. Your SEO job and your CRO job are the same: answer objections.
    </p>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Title:</strong> name + differentiator (material, model, use). Avoid internal codes.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Specs:</strong> dimensions, weight, compatibility, size chart, care instructions.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Proof:</strong> reviews, UGC photos, warranty, returns, real shipping times.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>FAQ:</strong> 5–8 common questions (delivery, sizing, durability, compatibility).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Internal links:</strong> link to the main collection + 2–3 guides.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>C. Trust pages: they support SEO indirectly</h3>
    <p>
      Shipping/returns, contact, about, warranty pages are not just legal. They reduce friction and can capture branded long-tail
      queries (“returns + brand,” “delivery time + brand”).
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section className="section" id="content">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="content">4) Content strategy in 2026: clusters, intent and proof</h2>
    <p>
      The content that wins for ecommerce is not “encyclopedic.” It helps people choose, compare, install and use—then buy.
      Think in <strong>journeys</strong>.
    </p>

    <h3>A. The 4 intents to cover</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Informational:</strong> “how to choose,” “how to care,” “what size.”
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Comparative:</strong> “X vs Y,” “best X 2026,” “alternatives.”
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Commercial:</strong> “X for [use],” “X premium,” “X budget” (often via collections).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Transactional:</strong> exact product/model/SKU (often via product pages).
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. Build a cluster (concrete example)</h3>
    <p>Example (adapt to your niche):</p>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Hub:</strong> “Complete guide to [category]” (criteria, use-cases, mistakes to avoid).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Supporting posts:</strong> “[category] for beginners,” “premium vs standard,” “size guide,” “care guide.”
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Links:</strong> hub ↔ supporting posts ↔ main collection ↔ hero products.
      </li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      Your hub should distribute authority to the pages that sell. Without links, a guide becomes an SEO dead end.
    </p>

    <h3>C. Keyword research: map queries to page types (Shopify-friendly)</h3>
    <p>
      “Keyword research” becomes actionable only when you map queries to the right page type. A common mistake is trying to rank a product page for a broad category term,
      or writing a blog post for a query that clearly expects a category page. In ecommerce, the SERP usually tells you what Google wants: if the top results are category pages,
      your best bet is a collection; if they are guides and comparisons, your blog/hub is the right format.
    </p>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Collections (category pages):</strong> commercial intent (“best waterproof hiking boots”, “running shoes for flat feet”). Write copy that helps choose and link to key products.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Product pages:</strong> transactional intent (“Brand Model 3”, exact SKU, specific color/size). Win with proof, specs and zero friction.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Guides (blog hubs):</strong> informational + comparative intent (“how to choose”, “X vs Y”, “best [category] 2026”). Their job is to <em>educate and route</em> traffic to collections/products.
      </li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      A simple workflow you can run every month:
    </p>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Step 1:</strong> list your highest-margin categories and your “hero” products (the ones you can keep in stock and ship reliably).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Step 2:</strong> pull real queries from Search Console and your store’s internal search (those are high-intent phrases your buyers already use).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Step 3:</strong> check the SERP for each query and assign the page type (collection vs product vs guide).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Step 4:</strong> for each money page, choose one primary keyword and a few natural secondary variants—then make sure internal links support that page (and not five competing pages).
      </li>
    </ul>
    <p className="note">
      Shopify-specific tip: don’t create “duplicate collections” to chase synonyms. Instead, keep one strong collection per intent and cover synonyms naturally in headings, filters (for UX) and copy,
      while using internal links from guides to reinforce the main category page.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section className="section" id="structured-data">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="structured-data">5) Structured data: Product, Breadcrumb, FAQ (and validation)</h2>
    <p>
      Structured data (schema.org) does not always boost rankings directly, but it helps with rich results (price, availability, reviews)
      and clarifies your page for machines.
    </p>

    <h3>A. Priority schemas for ecommerce</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Product:</strong> name, images, price, availability, brand, SKU/GTIN if available.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Breadcrumb:</strong> clarifies architecture (especially on collection/product pages).
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>FAQ:</strong> on guides or product pages (only if truly relevant—no spammy FAQ stuffing).
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. 2026 rule: validate, then monitor</h3>
    <p>
      Use rich result testing tools and Search Console “Enhancements.” A markup error can remove a rich result overnight.
    </p>
    <p className="note">
      Don’t add schema “everywhere.” Add it where it reflects real content. Engines penalise manipulation (fake reviews, artificial FAQs).
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section className="section" id="internal-linking">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="internal-linking">6) Internal linking: your SEO accelerator (and navigation guide)</h2>
    <p>
      Internal linking is the lever you control most. It (1) guides users, (2) guides crawlers, (3) transfers authority to strategic pages.
    </p>

    <h3>A. The 3 layers to build</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        <strong>Sitewide navigation:</strong> menu, footer, category links—stable and simple.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Contextual links:</strong> inside guides, inside collection copy, in FAQs and comparisons.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Related content:</strong> “read next” blocks—only when genuinely helpful.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. Anchor text rule</h3>
    <p>
      Good anchors describe the destination (“Shopify SEO guide 2026,” “waterproof hiking boots collection”) instead of “click here.”
      Don’t over-optimise: vary naturally.
    </p>

    <h3>C. A simple (but powerful) linking loop</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        “How to choose” guide → link to the matching collection.
      </li>
      <li>
        Collection → link to 3 guides (size, care, comparison).
      </li>
      <li>
        Product → link to collection + “how to choose” + shipping/returns policy.
      </li>
    </ul>

<section className="section" id="international">
  <div className="stack">
    <h2 id="international">7) International: Markets, language, hreflang (without traps)</h2>
    <p>
      International is one of the strongest growth levers—and one of the biggest duplication traps if mismanaged. The rule:
      <strong> one market = one clear offer</strong>.
    </p>

    <h3>A. Translation: intent and quality, not just words</h3>
    <p>
      Literal translation can miss intent. In 2026 engines are better at semantic equivalence, but they also detect low-quality pages more easily.
      Translate with local queries, units and logistics in mind.
    </p>

    <h3>B. Hreflang: your anti-cannibalisation insurance</h3>
    <p>
      Hreflang helps engines serve the right page for the right language/region. It is essential when you publish multiple similar versions.
      Make sure hreflang references are reciprocal (each version points to the others).
    </p>

    <h3>C. Currency, shipping, returns: SEO meets product</h3>
    <p>
      A page that ranks but can’t deliver to the target country creates frustration and weak engagement signals. Adapt key pages:
      shipping times, costs, return rules and taxes.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

    <h2 id="measurement">8) Measurement: Search Console + GA4 + ecommerce KPIs</h2>

      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Quality:</strong> returns, support tickets, reviews—SEO that sells badly (or disappoints) is not durable.
      </li>
    </ul>

    <h3>B. A simple operating cadence</h3>
    <ul className="list">
      <li>
        Monthly: top 20 SEO pages by revenue → pick 3 optimisations.
      </li>
      <li>
        Weekly: crawl errors / redirects / 404 → fix.
      </li>
      <li>
        Quarterly: content audit → update your pillar pages.
      </li>
    </ul>
